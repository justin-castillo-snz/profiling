# Establishment, kind-of-activity unit and local unit

Start with what the statistics must describe: **one activity, one place, or both**. Then ask whether the business keeps accounts at that level. [UN Guidelines](../sources/un-guidelines-on-sbr.md), §§4.5–4.6, paras 4.81–4.98; Box 4.6.

| Unit | What defines it | What does not follow |
|---|---|---|
| Kind-of-activity unit (KAU) | An enterprise or part engaged in one productive activity, or one whose principal activity accounts for most value added. | It need not occupy one site or correspond to one legal unit. |
| Local unit | An enterprise or part operating at or from one location; geography is defined for the statistical purpose. | A site need not have only one activity or separate production accounts. |
| Establishment (European equivalent: local KAU) | An enterprise or part at one location with one non-ancillary productive activity, or a principal activity accounting for most value added. | A distinct address alone does not establish that production, costs or investment can be measured there. |

This distinction matters most for large enterprises: one industry code for the whole enterprise can obscure significant secondary activities; one headquarters address can misallocate regional employment. If the books do not support establishment-level figures, the Guidelines discuss estimating from other information or retaining an unsplit enterprise, at the cost of less homogeneous industry data (paras 4.82–4.89).

**Choice of unit depends on the output.** The [2025 Wiesbaden presentation](../sources/wbg-statistical-units-2025.md), slides 5–10 and 13, points to the establishment's value for product and industry detail, while noting that some businesses cannot supply production, intermediate consumption, investment and employment at that level. Allen describes the establishment in shorthand as a part of an enterprise defined by activity and location (slide 6); this is not a replacement for the UN definition or the Canadian accounting test below. The slides cite [the Netherlands](../countries/netherlands.md) for establishment-based industry statistics and mention KAU use in EU short-term statistics, not a rule that either unit suits every output.

## Activity classification after delineation

Delineation decides **what the unit is**; activity classification decides **which industry code to assign to it**. In the [Wiesbaden presentation](../sources/wbg-statistical-units-2025.md), slides 15–16, “top-down” refers to the International Standard Industrial Classification (ISIC) method for finding the principal activity. The [UN Guidelines](../sources/un-guidelines-on-sbr.md), glossary entry “Principal activity”, explain that the chosen class must be consistent with its parent levels, using value added where available. The final activity need not account for a majority of the unit's value added. This is not the [top-down process for forming enterprises](../analyses/profiling-approaches.md#top-down-from-the-group-to-autonomous-enterprises). Secondary activity information or finer enterprise delineation, where justified, can retain detail without giving the same unit a second principal code.

**Cross-border production complicates the code.** Allen's example of a London office owning the raw materials and classified as manufacturing (slide 17) illustrates why the physical location of processing is not enough to determine the activity of the unit arranging production. It is not a general coding rule or evidence of UK practice. [Spain's case](../countries/spain.md) provides separate evidence of investigating economic ownership for complex production arrangements. Which classification rule applies depends on the unit, the activity and the classification version.

## The Canadian accounting test

[Canada](../countries/canada.md) identifies workplaces first. At each one, the profiler asks which inputs, revenue, wages and employment the business can actually report. A workplace can be a statistical location even if it cannot supply both operating costs and revenue. Where a single site has the requisite production accounts, it can serve as both location and establishment. Where several sites share those accounts, the Canadian establishment can cover several locations, each linked to its parent establishment. Location data still support subprovincial employment and GDP statistics. This is a **Canadian operational delineation**, not a change to the single-location ISIC definition above. (Annex F2, pp. 361–363, especially fig. F2.2.)

[Switzerland](../countries/switzerland.md) needs employment at workplaces, not a full production account for each. [Indonesia](../countries/indonesia.md) found that identifying meaningful operating and supporting units required industry-specific interpretation. For the separate question of who answers a survey, see [observation and reporting units](observation-and-reporting.md).
