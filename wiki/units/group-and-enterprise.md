# Group, control and enterprise

**Control in practice.** [Spain](../countries/spain.md) starts from consolidated accounts but explicitly seeks controlled units omitted from them. It then tests operating segments or lower-level business lines for decision-making autonomy. These are two decisions: belonging to the group is not sufficient to be a separate enterprise. [Eurostat LCU Guidelines](../sources/eurostat-lcu-guidelines.md), §6.6, p. 59. [Italy](../countries/italy.md) shows why even a known foreign parent is insufficient to classify a sale: the customer's branch may be resident domestically (§6.7, Annex 1).

A profiler needs two boundaries: **which legal units are under common control**, and **which combinations of units actually organise production**. The answers need not coincide. [UN Guidelines](../sources/un-guidelines-on-sbr.md), §§4.3–4.4, especially paras 4.36–4.49 and 4.70–4.80.

| Unit or relationship | What to establish |
|---|---|
| Legal unit | The legally recognised person or entity. Its tax registration or accounts identify a legal boundary, not necessarily an autonomous producer. |
| Enterprise group | The set of legal units linked under common control. Follow the chain to the controlling head, not just the immediate parent. A national or **truncated** group is the resident part of a multinational group; it may be as small as a single unit. |
| Enterprise | The producer with autonomy over its use of resources. One legal unit can suffice; several may need to be combined. If a legal employer or service company works solely for other units of the group, treating it as a separate producer can misrepresent their activity. The Guidelines qualify this for external sales and cross-border ancillary activity. |
| Consolidation boundary | The units included in a set of accounts. Evidence of control and evidence of accounting consolidation answer different questions: a partly consolidated joint venture, for example, need not belong to the control group (Annex F5, p. 372). |

**Enterprise wording across frameworks.** Allen's [2025 Wiesbaden slides](../sources/wbg-statistical-units-2025.md), slide 6, describe the enterprise as the smallest combination of legal units with a degree of decision-making autonomy. This complements the UN description above and Eurostat's emphasis on allocating current resources. The slide notes slight differences among the SNA 2008, ISIC Rev. 4 and EU formulations but does not give a crosswalk or settle a revised definition.

**Tests in practice.** [The Netherlands](../countries/netherlands.md) starts with control, checks consolidation, then tests operating segments for autonomy, external sales and reportable figures. [France](../countries/france.md) illustrates the reverse of combining companies: one legal unit could contain several autonomous enterprises in different sectors. [Colombia](../countries/colombia.md) distinguished the resident group from affiliates kept for a later group-wide investigation. See also [European profiling](../concepts/european-profiling.md) for what happens when the worldwide organisation and the nationally reportable units differ.

## Multinational limits and group types

- **National coverage.** A national SBR covers only the national parts of multinational groups; covering them entirely would need a supranational register and cross-country cooperation (§1.13).
- **Limited view of the non-resident part.** Statistical offices usually have only limited information on it, though surveys and commercial providers may help. The UN says the aim should be a shared view of each multinational, which is more feasible where collaboration programmes exist; European profiling addresses this (§4.60; Box 6.11; F1).
- **Group types.** The SBR can record groups as all-resident, multinational domestically controlled (global decision centre in the compiling country) or multinational foreign controlled (decision centre abroad) (§5.12). Truncated groups follow events at multinational level and do not have their own births or deaths in the same way (§§7.51–7.75).
- **Decision centre.** Usually the global group head, but a head without substantial presence, activity or employment may not be able to act as one, in which case the decision centre moves down the group tree. The UN says this cannot be set by an operational rule and "can be established during profiling", considering where main production, value added and R&D are located, and recommends agreeing it with other countries' offices (Annex A, p. 268–269).

## National scope and group-level classification (Stats SA, 2025)

- **Documented:** Stats SA profiles enterprise groups that start from an SA-incorporated holding company; ultimate holding companies incorporated abroad are excluded, and the abstract also excludes foreign subsidiaries. Within a profiled group, each company is classified by its own activity as a separate legal unit; no activity code is assigned to the group. ([Abstract](../sources/wbg-stats-sa-profiling-abstract-2025.md), p. 2; [presentation](../sources/wbg-stats-sa-profiling-presentation-2025.md), slides 5, 10.)
- **Analyst interpretation:** this is a national, resident-only view of the group, similar in effect to the truncated group above, but the sources do not use that term or describe enterprise delineation. It differs from the European manual's global delineation, where a foreign parent can be the starting point. The group here is a structural and relationship unit; the documents do not say whether enterprises or KAUs are derived from it.
- **Unresolved:** the presentation chart shows foreign subsidiaries, against the abstract's exclusion (see the source note).

## European manual: global delineation and autonomy

The [2020 Eurostat manual](../sources/european-business-profiling-2020.md), §§1.B–1.D, distinguishes the **global group head (GGH)** from the **global decision centre (GDC)**: the former is at the top of the ownership/control tree, while the latter is where strategic group decisions are made and determines which NSI coordinates European profiling. Neither should be assumed to be the other.

The manual's global enterprise (GEN) is delineated top-down by the degree of autonomy, especially over current resource allocation, not solely by activity or financial-reporting segments (§§3.A–3.E). It assesses managers and staff, reporting and cost systems, capital and production resources, operational decisions, and external or arm's-length transactions. A separate website, headquarters or management segment can be supporting evidence but does not establish a distinct enterprise on its own. Conversely, several LeUs can make one enterprise, and an LeU can sometimes be allocated across GENs, though the manual advises avoiding such splits where possible. The partner NSI makes the final national enterprise delineation, so global GENs and national ENTs need not have one-to-one boundaries (§§1.C–1.D, 3.D, 4.A).

The manual also describes special cases within its European scope: listed segments are delineated as GENs; financial activities in a non-financial group are placed in separate GENs (with holding-company activity treated differently); and a “Headquarters & others” GEN needs to meet enterprise criteria, including employing staff (§§3.C.4, 3.D.4). These are source-specific recommendations tied to the manual's 2020 European method, not universal rules for other statistical systems.

### Perimeter evidence and structural edge cases

The manual recommends checking the EGR perimeter against national registers, annual reports and the group's published accounts, distinguishing control from accounting consolidation (§§2.B–2.E). It notes that full consolidation, equity accounting and joint arrangements do not imply the same treatment: equity-method entities are outside its perimeter, while joint operations can affect turnover, employment and legal-unit inclusion differently (§2.D.4). It includes SPE legal units while retaining their control links, includes branches, and treats franchisees as independent enterprises outside the franchiser group (§2.D–2.E). These are the manual's European operational recommendations; local rules and source definitions must be verified.

Its sector examples illustrate why the autonomy decision is substantive rather than code-driven. In the profiled oil-and-gas cases, segments such as upstream, downstream and sales were generally separate GENs where they had their own management, capital and market-priced inter-segment transactions. In telecoms, bundled services and difficulty separating activities often supported one GEN despite activity classifications that might suggest several (§3.C.5, pp. 60–61). The manual does not attribute these sectoral cases to a named country.

## Unit vocabulary (INE Spain, 2025)

The INE paper uses LEU (legal unit), ENT (enterprise, "the smallest combination of legal units", citing Regulation 696/93) and GEG (enterprise group), plus three control concepts: the Global Group Head (top of the legal hierarchy), the Global Decision Centre (where strategic decisions are made, regardless of legal position or location) and the Ultimate Controlling Institutional Unit, which can be a natural person, a public administration, a sovereign fund or a resident or non-resident unit. GGH and UCI often but not always coincide; GGH and GDC may or may not. ([INE paper](../sources/wbg-ine-spain-lcu-paper-2025.md), §2.1, citing the Eurostat 2020 manual and the 2024 FATS and EGR manuals.)

- **Consistent with** the Eurostat manual's separation of GGH and GDC above, and with Allen's "smallest combination" wording. The UCI is an addition drawn from the paper's other cited manuals; its relationship to the UN's "controlling head" is not worked out in these sources.
- **Practical point:** ancillary and integrated units are excluded from a group's total output, and units not in the national register (branches, special purpose entities, some subsidiaries) are brought in during perimeter work. ([Paper](../sources/wbg-ine-spain-lcu-paper-2025.md), §4.3.)
