# European collaborative profiling

See the [approaches catalogue](../analyses/profiling-approaches.md#top-down-from-the-group-to-autonomous-enterprises) for the UN's dated list of countries that tested the method and for national adaptations of top-down delineation.

**Coordination is not delineation.** The [Eurostat LCU Guidelines](../sources/eurostat-lcu-guidelines.md), §§1.3, 2.2–2.3, distinguish the EuroGroups Register (group information), Interactive Profiling Tool (collaborative profiling), Early Warning System (voluntary early exchange on major events) and MNEnet (coordinators sharing knowledge and relaying it to domains). An alert can trigger investigation; it is not an approved change to a national enterprise. [Spain](../countries/spain.md) assigns monitoring of relevant restructuring events to its Profiling Area.

This is a **regional method**, not an account of what every European office does nationally. National profiling can start from the resident part of a group. European profiling starts from the worldwide group so offices observing different pieces can agree what they belong to. [UN Guidelines](../sources/un-guidelines-on-sbr.md), Box 6.11 and Annex F1, pp. 359–361.

## EWS and LCU adoption: 2025 Eurostat account

Eurostat's 2025 Wiesbaden paper describes the EWS as a structured, voluntary, non-legislative exchange between national compilers and Eurostat on restructuring events such as mergers and acquisitions. A group may be proposed for European profiling and monitored for a year or more, with the EuroGroups Register updated; closed cases are published as methodological summary notes. Obtaining information from the group is named as the main difficulty. The slides date the EWS to 2017 and report that over 20% of the 934 groups followed by national LCUs were also in Eurostat's European profiling programme in 2024. ([Paper](../sources/wbg-eurostat-lcu-paper-2025.md), pp. 3–5; [presentation](../sources/wbg-eurostat-lcu-presentation-2025.md), slides 4–5.)

LCUs themselves are voluntary in EU-EFTA. As of March 2025 Eurostat's 2024 survey counted 18 countries with an LCU, 4 with a similar structure, 4 creating one and 5 with none; the paper gave 18 "as of April 2024" (unresolved date difference). Start years run from Sweden 2004 to Austria 2024 ([presentation](../sources/wbg-eurostat-lcu-presentation-2025.md), slides 6–7). Reported legal basis for sharing confidential data is EU-specific (Regulation 223/2009 and 2019/2152) and does not transfer to New Zealand. *Analyst interpretation:* an LCU's existence does not show which unit-formation method an office uses.

The [2020 Eurostat recommendations manual](../sources/european-business-profiling-2020.md), §§1.C–1.D, describes this workflow as follows:

1. Establish the global enterprise group and its controlled/consolidated legal-unit perimeter. Validate the **global decision centre (GDC)**, which determines the lead NSI; it can differ from the **global group head (GGH)**, which sits at the top of the ownership/control structure (§§1.B, 2.C–2.D).
2. The GDC-country team uses desk research and group information to delineate **global enterprises (GENs)** top-down, then allocates legal units. This is a global analytical/technical delineation, not a national enterprise boundary. The **temporary enterprise (TENT)** is a technical country projection of a GEN, not necessarily the national statistical enterprise (§§1.C–1.D, 4.A).
3. Partner-country teams validate their resident legal-unit perimeter, attributes and proposed TENTs, then decide the final national enterprise (ENT) delineation. They may confirm a TENT or split, combine or otherwise reshape it to reflect national requirements. The GDC-country team likewise validates its own resident ENTs (§§1.D, 4.A).
4. The teams coordinate data collection, classifications and handover through a shared annual process; the manual's 2019 cycle is a dated example, not a current calendar (§4.B).

Manual intensity and selection are separate decisions. The 2020 manual describes **intensive** profiling as comprehensive dialogue including a meeting, **light** profiling as desk-based with absent or limited contact, and automatic methods for less complex cases (§1.B.7, p. 19). These terms belong to the manual's specific European framework; do not assume they match the [UN country's use of “light”](../countries/switzerland.md) or an LCU's monitoring activity. The A/B/C quality matrix described in the manual varies with group size and complexity; it is not a universal threshold.

### What the IPT holds

The 2020 manual describes the **Interactive Profiling Tool (IPT)** as Eurostat's secure collaborative platform for European profiling (§1.C.1; Executive summary, p. 10). It is best understood as linked, structured profile records rather than a public-facing profile report:

- **GEG:** group identity, GDC and the controlled legal-unit perimeter.
- **GEN:** proposed global enterprise identity, activity classification, employment and turnover, linked to its legal units.
- **TENT:** the IPT-generated national projection of a GEN from its legal units in a country.
- **ENT and LeU:** the national enterprise structure validated by the resident NSI, the legal units it contains, their identifiers and relevant attributes.

The manual's Chapter 4 describes attributes including unit names and IDs, NACE, reference year, source, employment type, turnover concept and currency; for legal units it also describes status, country, branch status and links or participation/consolidation percentages. The core measures are NACE, employment and turnover, but which levels and fields are mandatory or conditional varies by role and data availability (§4.C, pp. 71–77). Partners can flag changes and reshape their national ENTs rather than simply accept the GDC proposal (§§1.D.2, 4.A).

The manual reports that **26 NSIs collaborated** on 241 different GEGs during 2016–2018, resulting in 290 profiles including follow-ups (§5.C.2, p. 88). That is historical participation evidence, not a current country roster; the cited passage does not list the 26 NSIs. The profiling-calendar URL cited in §4.B currently redirects to a Webgate sign-in page (checked 2026-11-26). This explains why public search may not expose the workspace, but does not establish current platform access rules or which NSIs use it today.

**Why profile?** The manual links profiling to more relevant statistical units, improved measurement of economic concentration and activity, reduced double counting in non-additive variables, better observation-unit decisions and consistency across domains (§1.B, pp. 15–19). It argues that enterprise-level production variables and group-level strategic/financial variables answer different analytical questions (§3.B.2, pp. 50–52).

**National and European profiling have different starting perimeters.** The manual describes national profiling from the domestic/truncated group, while European profiling examines the whole GEG and coordinates country components. National priorities may differ from the European selection because a group can matter more to a country's statistics than to EU-wide aggregates; the manual recommends looking for synergies and sharing the European profile where relevant (§6.D, pp. 105–106). If a group's GDC is outside Europe, §1.F (p. 27) says the country with the greatest economic weight can coordinate where there is no European headquarters, and suggests light profiling may be more appropriate. This is a 2020 European method; global and temporary unit labels should not be assumed to map one-to-one onto register units or differently defined terms in other sources.

**Supporting infrastructure.** Box 2.8 (p. 41) describes the EuroGroups Register (EGR) as the SBR of multinational groups in Europe, built by Eurostat and national offices by pooling microdata on legal units, relationships, enterprises and groups, and supplemented by one commercial data provider for units outside the EU and EFTA. The ESBRs project (2013–2020) continues earlier EGR and profiling projects, aiming for an agreed profiling methodology, roles and responsibilities for countries profiling the same multinational, and a secure platform for sharing confidential data, with pilots. The UN notes that legal obstacles can hamper exchange of data on multinationals (Chapter 2, data sharing). Continuity of multinational groups is coordinated through the EGR identity number (§7.85). The project description is as of the 2024 text; its current status is not verified here.

[The Netherlands](../countries/netherlands.md) gives a practical reason for national validation: domestic units sharing a foreign parent may have neither meaningful combined accounts nor a reason to report together. Its country account uses *truncated enterprise* terminology; use F1 for the updated temporary-enterprise procedure rather than assuming an automatic equivalence.
