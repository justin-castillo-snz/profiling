# Canada

## Statistics Canada's complex-business profile

[UN Guidelines](../sources/un-guidelines-on-sbr.md), Annex F2, pp. 361–367; Box 7.3; Annex C3. These describe the Canadian Statistical Business Register (CSBR) as reported in the Guidelines, not a verified current manual.

**Two structures, linked.** Profilers build a legal hierarchy from tax records and majority ownership, and an operating hierarchy from the places where labour and capital are used. A location links to its parent establishment; each establishment links to its controlling statistical company. The enterprise directs domestic operations; the company is the level at which income, expenditure and balance-sheet accounts are maintained. The levels serve different accounting needs (F2, pp. 361–363, figs F2.1–F2.3).

**Ask about accounts, not just addresses.** Determine each operating unit's industry, workforce and, where available, revenue. Can costs and revenue both be obtained there, or only employment and partial accounts? The answer helps determine whether the unit is a location, an establishment or both. Several locations can share one establishment's accounts. Responsibility-centre language lets a respondent describe the business in familiar terms and tells survey programmes what can actually be supplied. The annex cautions that this granularity is expensive unless programmes need it. See [the Canadian accounting test and the UN definitions](../units/activity-and-location.md#the-canadian-accounting-test) and [reporting units](../units/observation-and-reporting.md). (F2, pp. 361–363.)

**Prioritise and scope separately.** Establishments are assessed by industry, geography, revenue and employment. A critical establishment brings its enterprise into annual prioritisation; that is not a requirement for an annual full-group interview. For a specific case, inspect update requests, discrepancies and profile age. One legal unit may need only its parent and operations checked. Group-wide disruption calls for checking all related companies; the account recommends a full group profile when the last one is more than three years old. (F2, pp. 363–365; [case selection](../concepts/case-selection.md).)

**Research before contact.** Annotate a working copy using tax registration and schedules, payroll, surveys, interviewer feedback, earlier decisions, gazettes and public filings. Check changes of control, consolidation, trading status, openings, closures, asset sales, and reporting capacity. Notify affected programmes of proposed statistical changes and let them challenge them. Contact the business only for remaining gaps; aim, where possible, to limit contact to once a year. Begin by phone with a marked-up structure and prioritised questions, directed to the person who understands the issue. (F2, pp. 364–366.)

**Validate before and after the live update.** Check allocated operating-unit revenue and jobs against tax totals up the hierarchy (*vertical coherence*); compare allocations with survey, programme and administrative figures (*horizontal coherence*). Check whether the combined units still support the enterprise's principal industry. Investigate discrepancies rather than manually replacing automated legal-entity financial fields. Apply updates, recheck and file the profile report. The register journal can preserve why a decision was made alongside a log of old/new values, sources and dates. Annex C3 describes the associated signal queue and structure manager; C3.6 adds a workload module that prioritises and assigns signals needing manual review to analysts or profilers, who implement or cancel the change request. (F2, pp. 365–367; Box 7.3; Annex C3.3–C3.6; [change and quality](../concepts/profile-change-and-quality.md).)

## The Enterprise Portfolio Management (EPM) Program (Voorburg Group, 2018)

[Statistics Canada paper](../sources/voorburg-statcan-epm-2018.md), pp. 2–7. A short programme description by the office itself, for a 2018 meeting; benefits are self-reported (see the source note's limitations).

**Scope and staffing.** About 300 of the largest and most complex enterprises (the BR holds about 5 million businesses; roughly 1% are "complex", meaning more than one operating entity). 10 Enterprise Portfolio Managers (EPMs, about 30 enterprises each), 5 analysts and 4 coherence specialists. Other complex enterprises are profiled by Business Register Division profilers (§§2–3).

**Selection.** Ranking by number of industries, number of regions and relative size (revenue, employment) in those industries and regions; updated every two years, then adjusted for feedback from subject-matter divisions and national accounts (§3). *Compare:* Annex F2 above starts from critical establishments by industry and geography. Both are Statistics Canada accounts; the paper does not say whether they describe the same selection or different tiers.

**One manager, several functions.** One EPM is the single contact for profiling, collection and other help; about 60% of EPM time is profiling. Coherence specialists are separate (§§3.1–3.4).

**Profile cycle.** In-depth interviews by phone or on site with senior staff; reviews at least every 3 years, earlier on signals; profiling questions mandatory under the Statistics Act (§3.2). *Compare:* Annex F2 recommends a full group profile when the last is more than three years old and aims to contact a business no more than once a year; the EPM paper describes an ongoing relationship with scheduled and signal-driven reviews. Different populations (all critical enterprises versus the 300 EPM enterprises) may explain this; the sources do not say.

**Accounts test and collection entities.** EPMs confirm how the business accounts for operating profit and assign the accounting type to each production unit; collection entities are derived from the profiled structure (§3.2). This is consistent with the accounting test in F2 ([activity and location](../units/activity-and-location.md#the-canadian-accounting-test)).

**Collection and coherence.** Combined questionnaires and a multi-entity spreadsheet tool; reported 95% response rate for EPM enterprises (§3.3). Pre-visit coherence reports; checks of establishment sums against tax control totals, monthly against annual, provincial against national; DataMart under the IBSP (§3.4). See [change and quality](../concepts/profile-change-and-quality.md).

**Training and tools.** Two-week course with test, task list, handbook and industry guides (oil and gas, head offices, property); Factiva API research (§4).

*Analyst interpretation:* the paper shows how one office joins relationship, profiling, collection and coherence in one portfolio role. It does not show cost, failures, or the effect against a no-EPM baseline, so it is a design to examine, not evidence that it solves each LCU problem. It also says nothing about foreign parts of multinational groups.

## EPM and Coherence Programs (NZ LCU meeting deck, 2025)

[Statistics Canada deck](../sources/statcan-epm-nz-lcu-meeting-2025.md), pp. 3–24, presented 26 Nov 2025 to a New Zealand LCU team. Self-description in slide form; no methods or thresholds.

**Scale.** 10 EPMs, 6–7 analysts and 7 coherence staff cover 230 ultimate parent enterprises (about 64,000 establishments; about 42% multinational). In 2020 they were under 0.1% of businesses but 46% of goods exports, 42% of employment, 28% of corporate value added and 27% of corporate revenues (pp. 3–4). *Compare:* about 300 in 2018 and 330 in 2015; the source note's [table](../sources/statcan-epm-nz-lcu-meeting-2025.md#comparison-with-earlier-statistics-canada-accounts) lists the figures. The reason for the fall is not stated.

**Selection.** Industries, regions and relative size, plus staff judgement on measurement problems (p. 5); see [case selection](../concepts/case-selection.md).

**Legal basis.** Statistics Act plus a Chief Statistician's prescription order making profiling by EPMs and BR profilers mandatory (p. 6). Whether NZ has equivalent authority is not established here.

**Relationship, profiling, collection, coherence in one team.** EPM as single contact who can restrict other agency contact at a respondent's request; structure information shared willingly is called a "must-have" for profiling (pp. 7–11). Profiling by research then mostly virtual visits with accounting staff, BR updates and Enterprise Reports (pp. 12–13, 24; [workflow](../concepts/profiling-workflow.md)). Collection entities customised per respondent, with 95%+ response claimed (pp. 14–15). Coherence analysis lists duplication, profiling errors, intercompany reporting, imputation and collection errors (p. 16; [quality](../concepts/profile-change-and-quality.md)).

**Lessons and hiring.** Management support up to the DG, a cross-survey data warehouse, clear guidelines (p. 17). EPMs need accounting, negotiation and organisation skills; coherence staff need accounting and programming; plan succession (p. 18). See [LCU coordination](../concepts/lcu-coordination.md).

*Open questions:* NZ's listed questions (p. 19) are unanswered in the deck; the "several metrics" for profiling cases and the omitted slides are unknown.

## Integrated Business Statistics Program (2015, historical)

[Statistics Canada overview](../sources/statcan-ibsp-overview-2015.md), §§1, 4, 6, 8–9. Written in 2015 while the programme was being built; **historical context only**, and much of it is plans ("will be").

**Setting.** The Business Register (BR) is the common frame for most economic surveys. Profiling is one of three inputs to it, alongside administrative data and survey feedback. About 99% of BR businesses were "simple" (one operating entity, industry and region); the roughly 1% "complex" businesses carried about 50% of revenue, and their structure was updated mainly by profiling and survey feedback because administrative data often lacked the needed level (§4). *Compare:* the 2018 paper gives the same 1% and over 52% of "total economic activity" (undefined); the shares are consistent in size, but the measures are not defined in either source.

**Four statistical units.** Statistical enterprise (often one legal unit, but for very complex businesses a family of legal units), company (lowest level for investment, profit, assets and liabilities), establishment (usually a profit centre with output, input costs and labour) and location (employment and/or revenue). Surveys target the level whose data the business can supply (§4). See [activity and location](../units/activity-and-location.md#the-canadian-accounting-test).

**Allocation factors.** Revenue and employment factors on the BR spread enterprise- or company-level reports to establishments and locations, mainly for provincial and territorial figures. Tax payroll data by province are the first source when complete and more current; profile variables are the fallback. Analysts validate the factors (§4). This shows why profile accuracy at lower levels matters when a business reports once at national level. See [observation and reporting](../units/observation-and-reporting.md).

**EPM in 2015.** 330 enterprise groups (several thousand establishments), about 90% response; EPMs keep profiles current, negotiate reporting arrangements, ensure coherence and act as single contact. A parallel agricultural programme (370 enterprises) also collects the data. Reporting for these businesses relied on spreadsheets that cost substantial manual effort and blocked early coherence checks (§8). *Compare 2018:* about 300 enterprises and 95% response ([Voorburg paper](../sources/voorburg-statcan-epm-2018.md)). The 2015 figure may include different scope or counting; neither source says. 

**Smart replacement.** For some complex businesses, administrative data may replace survey data when availability, accuracy, coherence and three years of operational and structural stability support it, reviewed case by case (§6). *Analyst interpretation:* profiling evidence on structural stability plausibly informs this test, but the paper does not say how.
